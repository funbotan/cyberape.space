Ever since humans started building machines, they kept trying to explain the mind by analogy with the fanciest machine they had at the time. Antique philosophers pictured it as a hydraulic contraption pumping humors around; the watchmakers of Renaissance imagined it as a clockwork mechanism; and with the advent of computers, only the laziest didn't try to fit the mind into a silicon mold. And while it's easy to see the naïveté of these views in hindsight, it's also hard to deny that with each technological leap, the artificial attempts at replicating intelligence look increasingly more similar to the real thing, and the better our replica, the more we learn about real intelligence from its limitations. As Richard Feynman famously wrote, [“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/8414-what-i-cannot-create-i-do-not-understand)

The further we go down this path of understanding through reverse-engineering, the wider the chasm between the materialist and spiritualist understandings of the mind, or at least so it seems to those on either side of the barricades. My goal with this article is to bridge this gap in one direction: to present a comprehensible yet faithful picture of what the materialist path has brought us thus far. And my hope is that, together, we can eventually build the bridge in the other direction.

## AI 101, the easy way

Without knowing anything about the nature of colors, we may naively assume that each hue (red, green, blue, yellow, purple, etc.) exists independently of the others. However, having started mixing colors, we eventually come to the conclusion that there are only three truly independent colors: red, green, and blue, and all the rest are their mixtures in different proportions, or, as we call them, *linear combinations*.[^1]

Knowing this, we can represent any color as a triplet of numbers by measuring how much red, green and blue components it has. We can also draw them as points in 3D space using each component as a coordinate in a corresponding dimension. Note that the same number of dimensions in the space of colors and in our physical space is purely coincidental; these dimensions have nothing to do with each other, although geometry works the same way in both spaces. The space of colors is an example of a *latent space*, and the triplets of numbers that represent colors are examples of *vectors*. Vectors that live in a latent space are known as latent vectors or *embeddings*. In general, a latent space and its vectors can have any number of dimensions, depending on what they represent. Often we draw vectors as arrows starting at zero because dots are not so easily visible.

![](%7Battach%7Dsilicon-mirror/colors.png)

But what is the benefit of this representation? Firstly, we obtain a correspondence between the physical process of color mixing and arithmetic operations on vectors. Secondly, this representation is also maximally *compressed*: it takes up the fewest possible numbers to represent a color because it has no redundant information.

In the case of colors, we knew the fundamental components in advance. But what if we want to build a latent space for other things where the fundamental components are not at all obvious: for example, for *words*?

Again, we can start with the assumption that all words are independent of each other, but this assumption will soon be shattered by fairly obvious examples of antonyms, such as “high - low,” “bright - dim,” “help - hinder,” “loud - quiet.” For each pair of antonyms, we can write down an equation of the form “high + low = 0”, “help + hinder = 0”, etc. In a latent space, such words are different directions in the same dimension. But we can go further: the right side of these equations needs not be zero. For example: “damp + cold = dank”, “irony + mockery = sarcasm”, “music + poetry = song”. Then, if we consider four-word equations, we can begin to illustrate the different kinds of relationships between words. For example, “king - man + woman = queen” is a semantic relationship, and “big + less = small + more” is a syntactic one. In a latent space, these would form parallelograms. And we can go further: there is no limit to the complexity of such equations.

![](%7Battach%7Dsilicon-mirror/geometry.png)

In principle, we could manually write down every possible word equation, express every word through the others, and use geometry to derive the optimal latent representations for them. Problem is, it would take so long that by the time we finish, the spoken language itself will change so much that we will have to do it all over again. This is why the problem of optimal word representation was solved only with the advent of machine learning. The solution came in a simply named [word2vec algorithm](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781). The general public might remember it from some meme-worthy examples of verbal equations:

> pig - oink + Santa = HO HO HO  
> pig - oink + Woody Woodpecker = Yabba dabba doo  
> pig - oink + Fred Flinstone = wassup  
> pig - oink + Homer Simpson = D’oh  
> pig - oink + Einstein = E = mc2

The details of the algorithm are not especially relevant for us today. The important part is that it takes a gigantic pile of human-written text and infers the relationships between the words simply by observing how they interact with each other. The underlying geometric computation is the same as what we would have done manually, just a lot faster and messier.

An interesting property of language is that embeddings of words from different languages will form a very similar geometric structure, and by overlaying the latent spaces of different languages, one can create a dictionary for translation between them *without a single example of actual translation*. This property has been known for a long time, albeit in a different formulation, and was used by archaeologists to decipher dead languages long before machine learning. It indicates that embeddings are not arbitrary but are an objective property (or rather, a homomorphic image) of what they are encoding. So, in principle, different methods for calculating embeddings of the same objects should converge to similar results.

![](%7Battach%7Dsilicon-mirror/translation.png)

However, you may have already noticed a problem with the idea of word embeddings: what if the same word has different meanings in different contexts? Is “bat” an animal or a piece of sports equipment? Is “plant” an organism or a factory? “Free” as in “freedom” or “free stuff”? The solution to this problem was a mechanism that takes a sequence of embeddings and selectively “diffuses” the meanings between them, clarifying each one. Perform this procedure many times over, and the individual meanings of words will eventually fuse into a cohesive whole: an embedding of the whole text that captures its general meaning without tying it to any specific words. This is how all contemporary AI systems “understand” what you ask of them, and they also develop this ability automagically by feeding on gigantic piles of human-written text.

In a similar fashion we can calculate embeddings for anything, provided that we have a sufficient number of examples illustrating relationships between the objects in question (training data). If you ever solved a captcha like “select all squares that contain X to prove you're not a robot”, you helped some corporation (most likely Google) create better image embeddings.

![](%7Battach%7Dsilicon-mirror/captcha.png)

The reverse of this operation, the un-embedding, is messier and differs between text and images, but its specifics are also not relevant for our purposes today. The important part is to understand that we have procedures to embed and un-embed data. Because embeddings capture only the general meaning, the un-embedding will not reconstruct the original data, but rather something that is meaningfully similar.

This is not unlike how, for example, the JPEG compression works. The raw photo captured by the camera, as well as the picture displayed on a monitor, are tables of millions of color values; but it is not stored in that form on the hard drive. Instead, the camera converts that table into special codes that are more than ten times shorter and stores those. When you want to display the image, the device you are viewing it from runs the inverse algorithm and recreates the color table. The pixels you see are not exactly the same pixels that the camera captured, but you'd be hard pressed to tell the difference.

However, embeddings have a crucial property that JPEG codes do not. Have you seen [videos](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=latent+walk) that consist of images endlessly morphing into each other, often in disturbing ways? [This is achieved](https://keras.io/examples/generative/random_walks_with_stable_diffusion/) by computing the embeddings between a starting and a final image, drawing a line between them in the latent space, taking points on that line with some regular separation and un-embedding each one in sequence (this process is called *interpolation*). But why stop at two? We can have three images and sample any combination of them. If we add another image, we get a three-dimensional latent subspace to sample from. That's where visual imagination is no longer of much help, but that doesn't limit computers, since they can process as many dimensions as can fit in their memory: for example, 59136 in case of Stable Diffusion. And LLMs can perform the exact same trick with texts instead of images.

The fact that such interpolation is possible means that there is a nearly infinite number of images or texts in between any two, and this number grows exponentially with additional dimensions. If each point in the latent space is a possible output of a GenAI model, then their number exceeds the number of texts and images that humans have ever written or drawn by a factor comparable to the number of atoms in the Universe. No wonder that many of them look original!

However, vast as it may be, the latent space mathematically cannot contain all possible images or texts: their numbers are even higher by orders of magnitude, and the vast majority are just noise anyway. So, what does it contain? It includes all possible things you can get by recombining the pre-existing data in various ways. All possible results of such recombinations taken together are called a *span*.

So then, what makes the creativity of GenAI different from that of humans? GenAI is locked within the span of the data it was trained on. This span is mindbogglingly large and has enough samples that will appear original to us. Obviously, not all human artwork is original either: most of it is also a remix of what came before. But a human can, at least in principle, create something outside of their span. If this weren't the case, it would be impossible for human creativity to ever begin because there would be no initial span for the very first artists or writers to sample from.

Of course, modern GenAI does much more than walk across the latent space: LLMs answer your questions, and image generation models create pictures from a textual description. But these abilities really just come down to doing fancier math in the latent space to find what the user is looking for more efficiently. The fundamental limit on GenAI creativity remains the same and will remain the same until we come up with a completely new technological paradigm for AI.

## Play stupid games, win stupid prizes

Most AI optimists will counter my previous statement with an argument like this:

> But we already have examples of LLMs solving mathematical problems that no humans solved before! They already achieve gold medals on the International Mathematical Olympiad! And even before that, we had other AIs that beat any human at chess, go and other games. This can't possibly be explained by recombination of human data alone.

And they would be right. But I will ask in return: why is it that LLMs exhibit borderline superhuman abilities to solve problems *specifically designed to be solved*, from textbook exercises to [the most complex math challenges ever made](https://x.com/__nmca__/status/1870170112290107540), yet fail miserably when confronted with real-life, open-ended problems without known solutions (as anyone who seriously attempted to replace human labor with LLMs will attest to)?

The answer to all above questions is that we are talking about two very different kinds of AI. In the previous section, I was talking exclusively about models trained in an *unsupervised mode*: they had an enormous amount of raw data and learned to reproduce it, building latent spaces in the process.[^2] The AIs that outperform humans at certain tasks are almost always trained in *reinforcement learning mode*: they are given the task itself and rewarded for completing it by any means necessary. And the AIs that score gold medals in olympic math are hybrids of the two: initially normal LLMs that are then further trained to solve mathematical and programming problems. Why these problems and not something else? For the same reason that previous AI systems like AlphaGo excelled at games: these are the areas with clearly defined rules, clear victory conditions and which can be cleanly contained inside a computer. There, in the digital realm, models can play billions of matches against themselves every day and make all sorts of stupid mistakes without real-life consequences other than power consumption. The sheer scale of this experience is then used to derive incomprehensible strategies that outperform anything that a human can learn in their short lifespan.

Another thing that can be clearly contained in-silico is physics. Physical simulations were some of the first use cases for computers, ever since the Manhattan project, and we've got pretty good at them. The easiest to model is rigid body dynamics: how systems of interconnected solid parts move under the action of external forces. On the other hand, building rigid-body robots is also fairly easy these days. Reinforcement learning allows us to combine these two developments: use a physical simulation, including a virtual body, to train a neural network how to control this body, and then just transplant it into a physical robot at the end. This setup is perfect for learning all sorts of physical stunts: [acrobatics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I44_zbEwz_w), [parkour](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tF4DML7FIWk), [martial arts](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1XCpBJn-Puc); but *it relies on reality not diverging from the simulation*. This is why all such stunts are performed in controlled environments, either with no human interaction or with trained actors who know exactly what not to do. Any environmental factors that are not mechanistically predictable will cause chaos. Self-driving cars are made the exact same way and suffer from the exact same limitations.

The AI industry leaders are currently busy trying to convince their investors that these limitations would magically go away if we just use even more data to train even bigger models. But they only have two ways of acquiring this data: either let robots roam the streets, sowing chaos and accidentally self-destructing while trying to learn how to accomplish basic tasks; or to continue training them in increasingly complex simulations. The former is obviously not a viable option for public safety reasons alone. The latter depends entirely on how well they can simulate the real world in-silico. However, the most important part of the real world that the robots would have to interact with is people, and if we could simulate people, we would already have had true AI and there would be no need for this simulation in the first place. So, it's a chicken-and-egg problem.

It’s hardly surprising that people are seeing LLMs cracking problems that only a handful of humans could solve before and assume that their performance on real-life tasks will be comparable. But this is a mistake that comes from treating intelligence as a one-dimensional quantity, a mistake that we have made many times before.

Remember when playing chess was used as a measure of intelligence? No? Me neither. I was born around the time that Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov, proving that winning chess does not actually require intelligence because Deep Blue clearly did not possess it. And [far worse metrics](https://youtu.be/30D00BDvfTA?si=iKETtHUZ8CY9H9jy) had been used before. I suppose that, in a decade or two, we will be viewing contest math problems as something not very different from chess: powerlifting for the brain, something you do purely for self-improvement. After all, we don’t hire powerlifting champions to actually lift stuff; we just use cranes.

## The truth and its interpretations

If you ask ChatGPT to solve some kind of problem without explaining the process and then ask to explain the process in the next query, the explanation you get will have nothing to do with the solution before. In fact, they technically cannot be related because LLMs have no internal state in which this information could have been saved. The explanation may coincidentally match the solution, but when it does not or it just doesn’t make any sense, we call it “hallucinated”. And the more I thought about this problem, the more I realized that it is not unique to LLMs: humans do the exact same thing all the time.

To see it, we need to expand our conception of *knowledge*. What we usually mean by this term in English is anything you can find in a book, film, podcast or forum and then store in your head. In Sanskrit, this is called **aparávidyá**.

But there is another kind of knowledge, the one that AI bros really want to brush under the rug because it does not fit their simplistic narrative about the intelligence of LLMs. This other kind is the subjective, internal knowledge that cannot be exchanged with the outside world. It is called **jiṋána**.

Whenever you attempt to smuggle jiṋána out of your mind, it simply turns into aparávidyá, and the process is irreversible. Even when an external piece of aparávidyá helps you understand something, that is only because you already had the jiṋána onto which it could map. Not all aparávidyá is linguistic, but all language is aparávidyá. And the central claim of the Indic gnosiology is that new knowledge can only be created as jiṋána.

It’s not easy to bring up examples of jiṋána precisely because it is, by definition, unobservable and indescribable, but here is an imperfect one I came up with. When you are trying to explain what you saw in a dream while still remembering it, your verbal explanation never quite matches what you actually saw. Like dreams, jiṋána is produced in the subconscious, with only the end result popping up into consciousness. This is how we get “eureka moments” or “shower thoughts”.

Jiṋána is the reason why we cannot actually teach people anything directly; we can only put them into situations where they are forced to generate their own jiṋána. Currently, the methodology and metrics of education systems are based on the assumption that the process of learning can be approximated as a mechanical accumulation of aparávidyá, but any teacher worth their salt knows that’s not even remotely true, that there is something much deeper going on. Ironically, the skill to teach people to acquire jiṋána is in itself jiṋána, but if we tried to at least think of it in the right way, perhaps we could come up with much better ways to organize the education process.

But what's most interesting for us today is the reverse process of forming aparávidyá out of jiṋána: that is, when people try to write down *interpretations* of their knowledge for others to use. While we, unlike LLMs, have a capacity for self-reflection and therefore the ability to explain what thoughts really led to our actions, this capacity is spotty and relies on having the right linguistic tools. This is less of a problem when interpretations are taught to people in tandem with practical skills, but it often makes the interpretation alone misleading.

A great example of this problem is mysticism. When we perform spiritual practices and read the corresponding literature, we can map the aparávidyá we receive from the latter to the jiṋána formed by the former. But without the practice, you'd never be able to make heads or tails of the literature.

What does this mean for LLMs? The idea that the AI bros are trying to sell the public on is that AI can learn human reasoning processes from the media that humans generate. But what we have just established is that not all data generated by humans, regardless of quality, can be trusted as examples of reasoning. If I had to generalize, I would say that *all interpretations*, meaning post-hoc explanations for practices, can potentially be “human hallucinations”. Even if only a small part of them really are, it could be enough to poison the dataset.

How much do humans hallucinate, and what percentage of training data out there can be classified as hallucinations? It is easiest to answer from contrariwise: to identify which data is definitely not hallucinated. The only texts that meet this criterion are the ones that are not interpretations, i.e., not written to explain something after the fact. But if you try to think about it, almost all text ever written is written after the thing it is describing had already happened. The only exceptions are the texts that are self-contained and do not refer to the material reality at all: mathematical problems with their solutions, some source code, and perhaps the most abstract strains of philosophy. An even smaller subset of that small subset, problems with objectively verifiable solutions, are used to train LLMs with reinforcement learning. So it is not coincidental that all instances of AI systems outperforming humans happened in self-contained problems with known rules: these are the only settings where we can reliably exclude human hallucinations from the training data.

Combining this with the conclusion of the previous section, we arrive at a picture that is very similar to the present-day state-of-the-art: LLMs excel at toy problems, exercises, and benchmarks but struggle to produce anything of real material value. And I don't see this picture changing as long as we stick to the current paradigm in AI development.

All this is not a limitation of deep learning itself. What I am really trying to say here is that language is not a *shortcut* to general intelligence. Moreover, I believe that the very idea of *general intelligence* is harmful because it is extremely susceptible to [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law): any metric chosen as target ceases to be a good metric. Worse yet, [if optimized too hard](https://sohl-dickstein.github.io/2022/11/06/strong-Goodhart.html), it ends up destroying whatever quality we were after in the first place. In the case of AI, this approach led to the “scale at all costs” mentality. In the case of humans, the measure of “general intelligence” is usually IQ, and since it is hereditary and can't be improved in adulthood, any attempts to maximize it eventually end up at eugenics. No wonder that the silicon valley AI bros found a common ground with the MAGA nazis.

## A quantum detour

While we are on the topic of interpretations, I'd like to touch on the potential connection between consciousness and quantum mechanics, even though it has no relationship to AI in its current form.

For the last hundred years, quantum mechanics has been a constant source of frustration for physicists and philosophers alike. Like all physics, it is a mathematical description of the world, and as such, it works remarkably well. Problem is, this description is completely alien to human common sense and to the way we perceive reality. In the world we live in, things have certain measurable properties, like location, speed, energy, etc. In the quantum world, all these properties are kind of “smeared”, they exist only as probabilities — or, more precisely, “wave functions” — instead of definite values. And we could have lived with this inconsistency if there was a clear boundary between the quantum world and ours, if we only had to unsheathe quantum mechanics to peer into atoms and such. But, as Schrodinger showed with his famous cat scenario, this was never going to work: we can easily find ways to amplify quantum effects to larger scales, meaning that these two worlds must somehow coexist. And we still don't know how they do.

To avoid going nuts from contemplating this dilemma, physicists had to come up with some story that made at least a little more sense than reality. And that's where the Copenhagen *interpretation* of quantum mechanics came from. It was created to force a separation of the worlds, even if there was none to be found in the math. This separation became known as “wave function collapse”: a hypothetical process that snaps the quantum soup of maybes into a definitive yes or no. And since we can only measure definitive values and not wave functions themselves, they theorized that the collapse must be caused by the act of observation. And what does the observation? [Only a conscious mind.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness_causes_collapse)

Incidentally, consciousness directly affecting reality (rather than through the mediation of the body) is the textbook definition of magic. And it did not take long for quacks and charlatans to latch onto the Copenhagen interpretation, inventing an entire branch of pseudoscience and pseudophilosophy collectively known as [quantum mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mysticism). And I find it very unfortunate that this garbage has infiltrated the Yogic community as well.

Even after a hundred years, no one has come up with an experiment that would show that the wave function collapse is even a real physical process, let alone what causes it. Neither was anything like it found in the math. Although the concept of collapse itself is not completely discarded in science yet, the idea of consciousness causing it very much is.

All this is not to be confused with the idea that Sir Roger Penrose proposed in his [1994 book “Shadows of the Mind”](https://archive.org/details/shadowsofmindsea00penr_0). I read it so you don't have to, and here is a very condensed sequence of his arguments:

1. Human consciousness is not reducible to computation because of Gödel's incompleteness theorem.
2. Since all physical processes we know are reducible to computation, the brain must be taking advantage of some other process.
3. The only process that fits the bill is the wave function collapse, because it is not physical and therefore not computable.
4. For the wave function collapse to have any macroscopic effect, the quantum system that collapses must also be macroscopic, i.e. the entire brain must be a quantum system.
5. The only structure in the brain that could form a macroscopic quantum system is the microtubule network.
6. Hence, human consciousness is a quantum process running on the microtubules.

This approach can be described as “quantum of the gaps” by analogy with “God of the gaps”, which means using God's will as an explanation for anything we don't currently understand. Each step in this sequence is extremely shaky, and the whole argument barely holds any water. But Penrose himself is well aware of it. Despite my somewhat derogatory style of presenting his idea, I actually hold a great deal of respect for the guy. He is an amazing scientist who contributed invaluably to mankind, and I can only aspire to be more like him. It's just that if you read between the lines of this book, it becomes pretty clear that he does not fully believe his own theory, but treats it more like an intellectual toy, just as a true scientist should.

Sometimes I also spot another idea being discussed: that free will is a quantum thing because quantum mechanics is non-deterministic. However, this one falls apart if you think about it for more than a second. Quantum mechanics is not deterministic, yes, but instead it's probabilistic: simply put, random. And randomness is no closer to free will than determinism.

There are also some people developing so-called “quantum AI”. What this almost always means is that they want to use quantum computers to accelerate the neural networks that we are already running on normal computers. Even if it works, such AIs will only differ from what we already have in speed, but they will be no closer or farther from being conscious.

## The simulation argument

While all public attention was on the generative capabilities of the new AI systems, their other applications slipped under the radar. And to me, these applications are much closer to science fiction than chatbots are.

One such application is [semantic reconstruction of thoughts](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-023-01304-9): decoding heard, read, and even imaginary (internal) speech from brain scans. Remarkably, the this reconstruction does not reproduce words exactly but in a slightly different formulation that preserves the general meaning, hence the “semantic” qualifier. This means that it captures *the thoughts* themselves and not, for example, the motor signals that the brain involuntarily sends to the tongue and larynx, even when it is not speaking out loud. But most interestingly, if the same system is used on a person who is watching a silent film, the result of decoding their thoughts will be a text description of the scenes of this film!

[Another experiment](https://ai.meta.com/blog/brain-ai-image-decoding-meg-magnetoencephalography/) achieves comparable results in decoding images, visible or imaginary. Again, the images do not match exactly, but they correspond in a rather curious way, reflecting what details the experimental subject is paying attention to. [Yet another similar system](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09350v1) was independently developed for peeking into dreams.

All these systems share a very similar architecture and contain two key elements:

1. Generative neural network. Semantic language reconstruction uses GPT-1, and image reconstruction uses an image generating model from the same class as Stable diffusion or Midjourney.
2. A model of the subject’s brain, implemented as an artificial neural network that, based on data (text or picture), predicts what signals the brain that thinks about this data will produce. I was amazed by the fact that this is even possible: in theory, modeling the human brain should not be achievable with the current level of technology. However, the models exist, and they work. Even if this is just a rough approximation at the moment, the very fact that such an approximation is possible and useful makes a big difference in estimating the complexity of this problem. Fortunately, to obtain results, the model must be trained individually on each subject, which is impossible without their cooperation, so it’s yet too early to worry about the adoption of mind reading by states and corporations for nefarious purposes. *Yet.*

The system works as follows. First, the generative model creates many different variations of text or images. These options are fed to the neural network that models the brain, and it generates corresponding signals. They are compared with real brain scans of the subject, and the most similar ones are selected. The data that generated these signals arrives at the output of the system as the decoded result, but at the same time is transmitted back to the generative model, which generates suitable continuations to repeat the process.

Mathematically, if we treat brain states as embeddings in their own latent space, then the process of mind decoding is just a similarity search in that space. Indeed, earlier studies independently confirmed that [the brain also uses the concept of latent space](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature21692), and [the process of memory consolidation in the hippocampus is strikingly similar to the computation of embeddings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/file/99064ba6631e279d4a74622df99657d6-Paper.pdf). Particularly amazing is a [paper showing equivalence](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.04035.pdf) between the hippocampus model and transformers, the class of artificial neural networks that underlie recent breakthroughs in natural language processing (that’s what T in GPT stands for) and which were developed without any prior knowledge of neurobiology. So, science has once again converged to a solution that nature had already invented.[^3]

Incidentally, some parts of our brains that handle the generation and perception of speech work in a way not unlike generative neural networks such as GPT, at least on the surface level. We [know for a fact](https://memory.ucsf.edu/symptoms/speech-language) that generating speech or text is handled by a different brain structure than analyzing it: Broca’s area and Wernicke’s area, respectively. This is what Hemingway’s famous advice “write drunk, edit sober” captures.

Perception is even more interesting: when you are listening to other people speaking, your brain [constantly tries to predict the next word it will hear](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-022-01516-2), which reduces the number of words that it needs to compare the sound to. This system is far more energy-efficient than comparing every sound to every word your remember, but it also comes with some quirks. For example, me and my current boss both speak three languages: Russian, English and German; and he loves to switch between them randomly. As a result, I often have trouble understanding what he says when he starts speaking because I don't know which language to *expect to hear*, even though I know the words he is saying regardless of his choice.

If you reflect on the subtleties of your own behavior, you may notice this predictive side of perception every time you interact with information in any way. Let's say, when opening an article, you may semi-subconsciously glance at the author; and if you already know them, then your brain will quickly make a prediction of what you are going to read about using the information it remembered from previous articles by the same author. This decreases the energy required to process the text and makes the reading process less frustrating. But this may also lead you into an echo chamber if you don’t make a conscious effort to expend more energy, much like with exercise.

This understanding of perception is also directly connected to Pratyahara, which I will illustrate with an example. I was once sharing a hotel room with a friend. He was sleeping and snoring while I was trying to meditate, initially with little success. But after some trial and error, I figured out that his snoring only disturbs me *if my brain is trying to anticipate* each sound. As soon as I was able to turn off this prediction circuit, I could properly concentrate and finish the meditation.

This all brings us to a question that has served as the go-to argument in the materialistic philosophy of mind for generations: *if we could emulate a human brain on a computer, would it be conscious?* If not, why? If yes, then what would stop other artificial intelligences created by different means from also being conscious? And sure, we may still be far from fully simulating a *human* brain in-silico, but we can already do it for a worm.
Indeed, scientists have [measured, modeled and digitalized](https://openworm.org/index.html) every neuron of a *C. elegans* nematode and are running copies of it as digital models or [robots](https://youtu.be/1wj9nJZKlDk), which appear to behave in the same way as a living worm would. If we believe all animals to be conscious, what does it mean for this model? And is it that different from a human from a philosophical standpoint?

## The silver lining

And there is yet another, even less publicized application for LLMs that, if it works, could turn out far more impactful than any possible use of their generative capabilities. Remember the observation that latent representations can be used to translate between languages without any actual examples of translation, or without any understanding of the languages on the part of the engineers? The wildest implication of this fact is that it is potentially not limited to *human* languages.

For as long as I remember myself, I was absolutely confident — and still am — that language is the only real cognitive difference between us and non-human animals. Even from a purely materialistic perspective, it is absurd to suggest that sentience, the crown jewel of evolution, suddenly popped up in humans along with the language cortex rather than developed gradually for hundreds of millions of years. The obvious implication is that sentience must be present in most animals with whom we share ancestry in that time period (which includes, at the very least, all vertebrates), and we have plenty of indirect evidence for it as well. Yet, this conclusion is avoided by mainstream science like the plague, and it's not hard to guess why.

While animals possess all human cognitive faculties except for language, LLMs possess *no* such faculties except for language. And it now appears that humans perceive LLMs as *more sentient* than animals. The first public indication of this was the scandal around Google’s LaMDA model and Blake Lemoine, who began to publicly [wonder](https://cajundiscordian.medium.com/what-is-lamda-and-what-does-it-want-688632134489) about whether there was a ghost in the machine. This was mere months before the release of ChatGPT, which made the same experience that Lemoine described available to everyone. Not long after, people began having relationships with LLMs and advocating for their civil rights. And this turn of events wasn’t unexpected: we knew [all the way back in 1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA_effect) that people read far more understanding than is warranted into texts strung together by computers. In other words, we can anthropomorphize anything as long as we can chat with it. Ascribing sentience to the machine is the null hypothesis that our socially adapted brains are automatically choosing, and the more proficient the machine is at stringing words together, the more expertise in the underlying technology (or, perhaps, the more spiritual prowess) it takes to see its output for something other than a product of genuine thought.

But what if I told you that the first sentence of the previous paragraph, that animals do not possess the faculty of language, was wrong? Ever since the publication of [Songs of the Humpback Whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songs_of_the_Humpback_Whale_\(album\)) in 1970, people have begun suspecting that cetaceans also possess full-fledged languages and not just signaling systems. Lots of popular media since then, from Star Trek 4 to Avatar 2, played into this idea. But it is only in this decade that the evidence really started coming together. My favorite [paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33726561/) on the topic analyzes old whaling ship journals for migration patterns of sperm whales and concludes that the only possible explanation for such migrations is that they can tell each other stories, meaning that they have not just languages, but full-fledged *cultures*. Languages are also suspected to exist in orcas, dolphins, and [humpbacks](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adq7055), although the jury is still out on the latter.

And if there are animals with their own languages already, then all we need to speak to them is a translator. And the technology to build such a translator is exactly the same as that which was used to build LLMs (although the data acquisition is much more difficult)! [Project CETI](https://www.projectceti.org/) is already attempting to decipher the language of sperm whales, and a similar effort was recently announced for [dolphins](https://blog.google/technology/ai/dolphingemma/). Imagine what will happen if these projects are successful. For the first time in history we may talk to someone who might as well be an alien (and in real-time too, unlike with the extraterrestrials). For the first time we may gain an outside perspective on ourselves, prompting us to rethink everything that seemed obvious. If the human tendency to associate language with sentience works just as it did for LLMs, then we will actually have to confront the fact that *we are not alone and never have been*. That event will bring about a true revolution: not in technology or politics, but in our own collective consciousness, from which everything else should follow.

And if whales have cultures, could they have their own spirituality, their own Guru, their own mantras? These are some of the most fascinating questions we can ask. And the answers to them may be just around the corner.
